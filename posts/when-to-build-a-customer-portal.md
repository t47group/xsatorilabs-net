A customer portal is a private website where your clients log in to see their own information: invoices, job status, documents, schedules, orders, messages. Many business owners have been told they need one. Some do. Some would be better off with a good email template and a shared folder.

Here is how we help owners tell the difference, and what we recommend building first when the answer is yes.

## The signal: the same questions, over and over

The clearest sign that a portal is worth building is a team spending real time answering questions that the business already knows the answer to.

- "Where is my order?"
- "Can you resend that invoice?"
- "What time is the crew coming?"
- "Did you get the signed paperwork?"
- "What did we pay last quarter?"

Each of those is a lookup. Someone stops what they are doing, finds the record, and types a reply. If your staff field dozens of these a week, and the information already lives in your systems, a portal can turn those calls and emails into something clients answer for themselves.

Count them for two weeks before deciding. It is a simple exercise and it gives you a real number to weigh against the cost.

## When a portal is probably not the answer

- **You have a handful of clients** and each relationship is personal. A portal can feel colder than the phone call they are used to.
- **Your clients will not log in.** Some customers — busy contractors, older clients, people who interact with you twice a year — will never remember a password. If the portal becomes another thing your staff has to walk people through, it has not saved anything.
- **The information is not in a system yet.** A portal displays data. If job status lives in someone's head or a whiteboard, the first project is getting it into software, not showing it to clients.
- **An existing tool already does it.** Your accounting software may already offer clients a way to view and pay invoices. Your scheduling tool may already send reminders. Turn those on before building anything.

## What a useful first version includes

When a portal does make sense, the first version should be small. In our experience, the features that earn their keep early are:

1. **Status at a glance.** The one or two things clients ask about most, shown on the first screen after login.
2. **Documents.** Invoices, contracts, reports, and certificates, downloadable without emailing anyone.
3. **Payments**, if you invoice clients, ideally through your existing payment processor.
4. **A simple way to make a request** — a form that lands in the right person's queue with the client and account already attached.
5. **Notifications** by email or text when something changes, so clients do not have to check.

What usually waits for a later phase: in-portal chat, detailed analytics, custom dashboards for each client, and mobile apps. They are often requested and rarely used as much as expected in the first year.

## Logins are the hardest small detail

The login experience decides whether clients use a portal at all. A few choices help:

- **Magic links or one-time codes** sent by email or text, so clients do not need to remember another password.
- **Multiple users per account.** Your client's office manager and owner may both need access, with different permissions.
- **Invitations sent by your team**, not self sign-up, for most business-to-business portals. You control who gets in.

And because clients will see financial and operational information, the security basics matter: strong authentication, permissions enforced on the server, and logging of who viewed and downloaded what.

## It is really an integration project

Most of the cost in a portal is not the screens clients see. It is connecting those screens to the systems where the information actually lives — accounting, scheduling, job management, document storage.

That has two consequences. First, the portal is only as accurate as the data behind it. If job status is updated once a day, the portal is a day behind. Second, the integration choices you make now will shape what is easy to add later. A portal that reads from a well-organized internal system is straightforward to extend. One that scrapes three disconnected tools is fragile.

This is the same problem we work on in internal software generally: one place that pulls together what the business already runs on. A portal is often that same hub, with a client-facing window on part of it.

## Questions to answer before you scope it

- What are the five questions clients ask most often, and how many times a week?
- Where does the answer to each one live today?
- Who on the client side would log in, and how often?
- Do any of your current tools already offer a client view you have not turned on?
- What would you want to change about the portal in a year, and who would make that change?

## Start with one question

A good way to test the idea is to build the portal around a single question — usually "where is my order" or "show me my invoices" — and see whether clients use it. If they do, the next feature is easy to justify. If they do not, you have learned something useful without paying for a large system.
