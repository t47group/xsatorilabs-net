Phoenix has a real software scene now — agencies, studios, independent contractors, and offshore shops with a local salesperson. If you are a business owner here trying to get something built, the hard part is not finding options. It is telling them apart, because every one of them has a good-looking website and a portfolio page.

Here is what we would actually ask if we were on your side of the table.

## About the work itself

**1. Who specifically is writing my code?** Not the company — the people. Sales engineers who disappear after signing are the oldest pattern in this industry. Ask to meet the actual team.

**2. Have you built something with this shape before?** Not the same industry, the same *shape*. A marketplace is a marketplace whether it moves freight or furniture. Industry familiarity is nice; structural experience is what saves you money.

**3. What will you tell me not to build?** A studio that agrees with everything is optimizing for the sale. The most valuable thing we do for clients is talk them out of features, and any studio worth hiring should have opinions about your scope within twenty minutes.

**4. What is the riskiest part of this project?** If they cannot name one, they have not thought about it, and you will find it together at the worst moment.

## About ownership

**5. Who owns the code?** You. In writing. If this is ambiguous, walk.

**6. Whose accounts are the services under?** Hosting, domains, databases, app store listings — all should be yours, with the studio invited in. Businesses get held hostage this way regularly, and it is entirely preventable at signing.

**7. What happens if we stop working together next month?** A good answer describes a handoff: documented, deployable, transferable. A vague answer means you are buying dependency.

## About money

**8. What is not in this quote?** Usually: data migration, admin tooling, content, third-party fees, app store accounts, and post-launch support. Get the list.

**9. What does it cost annually after launch?** Hosting, services, maintenance, updates. Never zero. A studio that has not told you this number has left you with a surprise.

**10. What happens when I change my mind?** You will. Ask for the actual process and the actual cost, before it is happening.

## About how they work

**11. How often will I see working software?** Weekly is reasonable. Monthly is slow. "At the end" is a red flag — long stretches without a demo are where projects go quietly wrong.

**12. What do you need from me, and how much of it?** Every project needs client time for decisions, content, and review. A studio that claims to need almost none of yours is planning to guess, and you will not like the guesses.

**13. Can I talk to a client whose project went badly?** Everyone has one. How a studio talks about a difficult project tells you more than any success story — you are learning what happens when things go wrong, which they will.

## Two questions about local

**14. Does local actually matter here?** Sometimes genuinely yes: if the work requires being in your facility, understanding local regulation, or frequent in-person sessions. Sometimes it is just preference, which is fine — but know which one you are paying for, because local usually costs more.

**15. If you use offshore or contract talent, tell me now.** Plenty of good studios do, and it is not a problem. Discovering it in month three is.

## What we would add

Ask for a small paid engagement before a large one. A two-to-four week discovery phase — technical approach, real scope, an estimate someone will stand behind — costs a fraction of the build and tells you almost everything about whether you want to work with these people. You learn how they communicate, whether they push back, whether they deliver what they said on the day they said.

If the answer after four weeks is that you would rather work with someone else, that is the cheapest possible version of that lesson.
