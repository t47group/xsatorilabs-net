A few months after new software launches, most owners have an impression of how it is going. The team seems to like it, or grumbles about it. Things feel faster, or they do not. That impression is worth something, but it is a weak basis for deciding whether to invest more, change direction, or leave things as they are.

As the year closes and 2027 plans take shape, it is a good moment to look at the software you added this year with a little more rigor. Here is how we help clients measure whether a system is doing its job.

## Start with why you built it

Every software project starts with a reason: hours spent retyping data, orders that slipped through, reports that took days, customers who waited too long. Go back to that reason and write it down as something you can count.

- "Scheduling takes too long" becomes hours per week spent building the schedule.
- "We lose track of jobs" becomes jobs per month that miss a deadline or get billed late.
- "Month-end is a nightmare" becomes days from month-end to closed books.
- "Customers keep calling for updates" becomes status calls per week.

If you did not measure these before launch, all is not lost. Staff memory, old timesheets, email volume, and records in the old system can usually produce a reasonable estimate. A rough baseline is far better than none.

## Measure adoption first

Software only pays off if people use it. Before looking at results, check whether the system is actually being used the way it was designed.

Useful signs:

- **Are the intended people logging in?** Most systems can show active users by week.
- **Is work happening in the system, or around it?** If the old spreadsheet is still open on everyone's screen, the new system has not replaced it.
- **Which features are used, and which are ignored?** A feature nobody touches is either unnecessary or hard to find.
- **Where do people fall back on workarounds?** Side notes, separate spreadsheets, and "I just call them" are signals of a gap.

Low adoption is not necessarily the software's fault. It may be a training problem, a missing feature, or a process that was never fully switched over. But it has to be solved before anything else can be measured.

## Then measure the outcome

Once people are using the system, compare the numbers to your baseline. Look at:

**Time.** How long does the task take now? Ask the people who do it, and where possible, use timestamps from the system itself — when a job was created, scheduled, completed, and billed.

**Errors.** Are fewer things going wrong? Billing corrections, missed appointments, duplicate records, and rework are all countable.

**Speed of the business.** How quickly do things move from start to finish? Time from inquiry to quote, order to delivery, job completion to invoice, invoice to payment.

**Capacity.** Can the same team handle more work? This is often the biggest benefit and the one least likely to show up on its own, because it appears as growth that did not require a new hire.

## Put a dollar figure on it, roughly

You do not need a precise return-on-investment calculation. You need a credible range.

Hours saved multiplied by a loaded hourly cost gives a staff-time figure. Fewer billing errors and faster invoicing affect cash. Fewer missed appointments or lost orders affect revenue. Add those up, compare them to what the software cost to build and what it costs each year to run, and you have a defensible answer to "was it worth it?"

Be honest about the costs, including hosting, subscriptions, maintenance, and the internal time it took to roll out.

## Build measurement in from the start

The easiest way to measure software is to design it to be measured. On projects we build, we try to agree on the handful of numbers that define success during scoping, and make sure the system records what is needed to report on them: timestamps for key steps, status changes, and who did what.

That does not mean elaborate dashboards. A short monthly report with five numbers is usually more useful than a screen with fifty charts nobody reads.

## Listen to the people using it

Numbers tell you what is happening. The people using the system tell you why. A short conversation with a few users every quarter — what is easier, what is still annoying, what they wish it did — is the best source of ideas for what to improve next.

Pay particular attention to small frustrations that happen many times a day. Fixing a slow screen or an extra click that everyone hits fifty times a shift can be worth more than a new feature.

## Decide what happens next

With adoption, outcomes, and user feedback in hand, the decision usually falls into one of four categories:

1. **It is working. Leave it alone** and keep it maintained.
2. **It is working, with gaps.** Fix the specific issues users raised.
3. **It is working, and the next problem is clear.** Use the same approach to tackle the next item on your list.
4. **It is not working.** Figure out whether the problem is adoption, process, or the software itself before spending more.

Each of those is a reasonable outcome. The mistake is not making the decision at all and letting the system drift.

## A year-end exercise

For each significant system you added or changed this year, write one paragraph: what it was supposed to fix, what the numbers say now, what users say, and what you will do about it in 2027. That page becomes a useful input to next year's budget.
