October and November are when most businesses set next year's budget. For software, this is also the season when good intentions turn into a line that reads "new system — TBD" and then sits untouched until spring.

If there is a software project you expect to tackle in 2027, the fourth quarter is the right time to do the thinking that makes the money useful. Here is how we would approach it.

## Start with the problem list, not the project list

Before naming any software, write down the operational problems that cost you the most this year. Hours spent retyping data. Orders that slipped through. Reports that took days to assemble. Customers who left because something was slow or confusing.

Then estimate, roughly, what each one costs per year in staff time, errors, and lost revenue. You do not need precision; you need a ranking. The problem at the top of that list is where your software budget should start, and the estimate gives you a ceiling on what it is worth spending to fix it.

## Use the rest of this year for discovery

The most common reason software projects start late is that the first month of the new year gets spent figuring out what to build. If you do that work now, you can start building in January instead of March.

A short discovery engagement in Q4 typically covers mapping the current process, identifying the integrations, naming the risky parts, and producing a scoped plan with an estimate someone will stand behind. It is a small fraction of the build cost, and it turns a budget placeholder into a real number.

For some businesses, there is also a practical reason to spend before December 31. Talk to your accountant about how software development costs are treated for your business; the rules have changed in recent years, and the answer depends on your situation.

## Fund it in phases

Large software budgets approved as a single lump tend to go wrong in a predictable way: the plan made in October gets followed through to the following September, long after reality has changed.

A phased budget holds up better:

- **Phase one:** the smallest version that solves the top problem. A defined scope and a firm budget.
- **Phase two:** improvements based on how people actually use phase one.
- **Phase three:** the next problem on the list.

Approve phase one. Earmark funds for the rest, but release them only after phase one ships and you can see whether it worked. This keeps the business in control of the money and makes each decision with more information than the last.

## Budget for the full year, not just the build

A realistic 2027 software line includes more than development:

- Hosting and third-party services, from launch onward.
- Ongoing maintenance and small changes.
- Your own team's time for decisions, testing, and training. This is the cost most often left out, and it is real.
- Data migration and cleanup from the old system.
- A contingency for the things nobody anticipated. There will be some.

If you already have custom software, include its maintenance as its own line. Systems that go a year without updates tend to need expensive catch-up work later.

## Line up a partner before January

Studios fill their early-year schedules in the fall. If you want to start building in Q1, the conversations should happen now. That gives you time to compare proposals carefully rather than signing with whoever can start soonest.

For Phoenix and Arizona businesses, this is also a good time to meet potential partners in person, see how they work, and ask the questions that matter before a deadline is pressing on you.

## A Q4 checklist

1. List the three operational problems that cost the most this year.
2. Estimate a rough annual cost for each.
3. Choose one to solve first.
4. Run a short discovery phase before year-end if possible.
5. Approve phase one with a firm budget; earmark later phases.
6. Include maintenance, hosting, and internal time in the budget.
7. Talk to your accountant about timing.
